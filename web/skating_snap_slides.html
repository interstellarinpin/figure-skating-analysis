The Snap

The physics of a figure skating jump,
and why it should feel effortless

Part 2 of The Falling Cat · → / space / click to advance · F for fullscreen

From the cat to the skater

The falling cat

  • \(L = 0\): nothing to push off
  • Turns by changing shape only: \(\;\dtU = \cS\,\dPS\)

The skater

  • \(\LL \neq 0\): taken from the ice at takeoff
  • Spins and changes shape
Same two-body physics, plus one extra term. Shoulders and arms are the upper body; hips and legs are the lower body.

Watch the snap

1. On the ice, the upper body opens ahead of the hips.

2. The pick: the toe plants and the weight transfers onto the right foot. Then the blade leaves the ice with the shoulders still leading.

3. The arms draw in, and the lower body swings into line with the shoulders.

Step 3 is the snap. It looks effortless because the skater barely does anything to cause it. Let's derive why.

A two-body skater

Both halves spin about the same vertical axis.

\(\tU,\ \tL\): which way the shoulders and hips face.

\(\PS = \tU - \tL\): how far the shoulders lead the hips.

\(\sv\): arm position, \(0\) (pulled in) to \(1\) (out). It sets \(\IU(\sv)\); \(\IL\) is fixed.

\(\PS\) = 0.00 \(\sv\) = 1.00 \(\IU\) = 1.20

Angular momentum, now not zero

\[\LL = \IU\,\dtU + \IL\,\dtL\]
\(\LL\) is taken from the ice at takeoff, then stays constant in the air
\[\dtU = \dtL + \dPS\]
Same twist as the cat: \(\PS = \tU - \tL\)
\[\LL = \IU\,\htmlClass{hl}{(\dtL + \dPS)} + \IL\,\dtL\]
Substitute
\[(\IU + \IL)\,\dtL = \LL - \IU\,\dPS\]
Collect the \(\dtL\) terms
\[\dtL = \frac{\LL}{I} - \frac{\IU}{I}\,\dPS, \qquad I = \IU + \IL\]
Divide by the total inertia
\[\boxed{\;\dtL = \underbrace{\frac{\LL}{I(\sv)}}_{\color{#5CD0B3}\text{dynamic spin}} + \underbrace{\kS\,(-\dPS)}_{\color{#F0AC5F}\text{the snap}}\;}\qquad \kS = \frac{\IU(\sv)}{\IU(\sv) + \IL}\]
The hips' rotation rate splits into two terms
\[\LL = 0:\quad \dtU = \dtL + \dPS = (1 - \kS)\,\dPS = \cS\,\dPS\ \checkmark\]
Turn off the spin and you get the cat back, with \(\cc = 1 - \kk\)

From rate to net turn

\[\Delta\tL = \int \dtL\,dt\]
Add up the hips' turning rate over the flight
\[\phantom{\Delta\tL}= \int \frac{\LL}{I}\,dt \;+\; \int \kS\,\Big(-\frac{d\PS}{\cancel{dt}}\Big)\,\cancel{dt}\]
In the snap term the \(dt\)'s cancel, just as for the cat
\[\phantom{\Delta\tL}= \underbrace{\int \frac{\LL}{I}\,dt}_{\color{#5CD0B3}\text{depends on timing}} \;+\; \underbrace{\int \kS\,(-d\PS)}_{\color{#F0AC5F}\text{depends only on the path}}\]
Pulling in raises \(\LL/I\) (the familiar part)
\[\text{snap} = \int_{\htmlClass{hl}{\text{takeoff}}}^{\text{landing}} \kS\,(-d\PS) \;\approx\; \bar{\kk}\;\PS(t_{\text{takeoff}})\]
Only the lead still stored when the blade leaves the ice gets converted
Time drops out of the integral, but takeoff sets its limits. As long as the blade is on the ice, it holds the hips, so nothing converts, even after the pick while the weight is still transferring onto the right foot. Leaving the ice releases whatever shoulder lead \(\PS(t_{\text{takeoff}})\) is stored at that instant. When you open the shoulders matters a lot.

Timing: takeoff releases the snap

On time: the shoulder lead peaks right as the blade leaves the ice. All \(0.9\) rad is stored when the hips are released, giving the full snap.

Too early: the shoulders start unwinding while still on the ice. The blade holds the hips, so that part of the lead is wasted. Less is stored at takeoff.

Too late: the shoulders are still opening after takeoff. Opening in the air pushes the hips backward by \(\kk_{\text{out}}\) per radian, which eats into the snap.

Conservation of angular momentum is the tip of the iceberg

The snap, in the air

\(t\) = 0.00 s\(\PS\) = 0.90 \(\sv\) = 1.00snap: 0.00 rad
Riding along with the spin, the hips swing forward 0.38 rad (22°) and the shoulders check back to meet them. Nobody pushed: it's internal.
The landing leg turns 22° more than \(\LL\) alone would give it, without any extra angular momentum.

The snap is an area

Plot the snap in the \((\PS, \kk)\) plane. The shoulders' lead \(\PS\) closes from \(0.9\) to \(0\).

Arms drawing in during the snap: area \(= \int \kk\,(-d\PS) = 0.38\) rad \(\approx 22^\circ\)

Arms already in: \(0.259 \times 0.9 = 0.23\) rad \(\approx 13^\circ\)

Arms still out: \(0.545 \times 0.9 = 0.49\) rad \(\approx 28^\circ\), but wide arms make \(\LL/I\) smaller, which slows the spin.

Snap as the arms come in: you keep most of the snap, and the spin speeds up at the same time.

The wind-up: the blade holds the hips

\[\IU\,{\color{#58C4DD}\ddot\theta_U} = \tau_m \qquad \IL\,{\color{#F0AC5F}\ddot\theta_L} = -\tau_m + \tau_{\text{ice}}\]
The waist muscles turn the shoulders one way and the hips the other, with equal and opposite torque \(\tau_m\)
\[\text{blade grips:}\quad {\color{#F0AC5F}\ddot\theta_L} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \tau_{\text{ice}} = \tau_m\]
The ice pushes back on the hips exactly as hard as the waist pulls on them
\[\ddot{\PS} = \frac{\tau_m}{\IU}, \qquad \Delta\tL = 0\]
The whole twist goes into the shoulder lead. The hips give up no ground.
\[\PS(t_{\text{takeoff}}) = \PS_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{snap} \approx \bar{\kk}\,\PS_0 = 0.38\ \text{rad}\]
The lead is banked as shape. Takeoff releases the hips and it converts into rotation.
Take the ice away (\(\tau_{\text{ice}} = 0\)) and the same waist torque splits the twist: the hips swing back by \(\kk\,\PS\). The cat has that problem on both halves of its loop. The skater's blade pays for the wind-up half, so the whole snap is a net gain.

Why not just turn the whole body?

\[\frac{d\LL}{dt} = \tau_{\text{ice}} + \underbrace{\tau_{U \to L} + \tau_{L \to U}}_{=\,0}\]
Torques between your own body parts come in equal and opposite pairs
\[\text{rigid body } (\dPS = 0):\quad \Delta\theta = \int \frac{\LL}{I}\,dt \text{ only}\]
Every degree has to come from \(\LL\), and \(\LL\) only comes from the ice
\[\tau_{\text{ice}} = \vec r \times \vec F_{\text{blade}}\]
More torque from the ice means bigger sideways forces at the blade. Any misalignment with your center of mass also tilts the spin axis: a balance problem.
The snap is an internal torque: no push from the ice, no change in \(\LL\), nothing to tip the axis. That's why it looks effortless.

The new mindset

Stop trying to force the whole body around. Physics already delivers the rotation. A skater only has to manage two things:

1

Balance

Stay stacked over the blade at takeoff. The ice can give you \(\LL\) and hold your hips for the wind-up, but only if you're on axis.

2

Create the snap with the upper body

Open the shoulders on the ice so the lead peaks right as you leave the ice. Takeoff releases it, and the hips catch up on their own: \(\bar{\kk}\,\PS(t_{\text{takeoff}})\).

Two repeatable actions instead of a full-body heave \(\;\Longrightarrow\;\) a lot more consistency.

On the ice

Summary

1. \(\dtL = \dfrac{\LL}{I} + \kS(-\dPS)\): dynamic spin plus the snap

2. The snap \(\approx \bar{\kk}\,\PS(t_{\text{takeoff}})\): only the lead stored when the blade leaves the ice counts, so timing matters, not effort

3. Wind up on the ice (the blade holds the hips), snap in the air: \(\approx 22^\circ\) for free in this model

4. Internal torque can't tip you over. Forcing a rigid turn off the ice can.

5. Focus on two things: balance and the upper-body snap
